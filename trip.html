Trip Archive

I started this page in May 2026 to share my trips outdoors: hikes, backpacking, long wilderness days, and sightseeing trips that do not need to be physically hard at all. I have loved scenic views since I was young, but I did not really spend time in the wilderness until I moved to Seattle for my PhD. Growing up in Beijing, I “climbed” the usual paved hills and did the usual tourist trips in China; looking back, they were closer to walking stairs in a well-kept park. Here I started with trails around the city and suburbs, then, once I had a car, the state parks and forests farther out. Lakes, forests, and the coast all pulled me in, but mountains took hold of me the most.

The summer after I finished my Master’s degree, I went to Yunnan and stood below Yulong Xueshan (玉龙雪山). It was another tourist trip, but we were at 4600m and it felt so real. The weather was rough: heavy cloud, a whiteout, almost no view from the high places. Still, the granite walls and the shapes the glaciers had cut left me speechless. Since then I have wanted to see more mountains up close, and maybe stand on top of a few, especially ones with snow and ice. And yes, crazy as it sounds, I also dream of an eight-thousander someday, if money, body, and luck allow.

In Washington I loved green hills like Mount Si, but over time I found myself wanting to go higher. Looking at alpine peaks, I wanted to be on top, not only on the trails around them. In the North Cascades there are 2000m glaciated peaks you can reach without altitude sickness; from the road and from the plane, snow-capped volcanoes like Mt. Rainier and Mt. Baker keep showing up. Looking at them, I keep imagining a ridge underfoot and how small a person would look against those flanks. That scale is hard to shake.

I asked around for summits that were not too technical, preferably with snow and glaciers. On a hitchhike back down from Hidden Lake Lookout, a former local mountaineer told me Ruth Mountain might be a good start, with no crevasses to worry about (actually there are); later I checked with Galen, a true mountain person who never tires of my endless beginner questions. It took me two attempts (early season is honest about how unready you can feel). When I finally stood up there, I realized I could stand where I had once only stared. If I could summit Ruth with average fitness and almost no skills, nothing could hold me back, especially since I can always learn from failing. After that I planned, failed, and summited more alpine peaks in this state that season, each with its own memory and its own kind of challenge. I do not know whether I will ever go very technical, and I have little interest in being super fast. What keeps me going is moving through the scenery under my own power, hardship included, and actually feeling the beauty of the landscape along the way.

I believe many things are destined to be, like what brought me to Washington and back to my love for the outdoors. Do I have a bucket list? Not really. If it’s meant to be, it will be. Lately I am more drawn to places where glaciers are dying or recently gone, places with interesting geologic features, and Washington’s fire lookouts. All of them feel like pieces of history.

For the most part, I think of myself as a hiker who loves mountains and challenging adventures, but you will also find easy walks, scenic drives, and trips that are just about seeing somewhere beautiful. Some of these trips also live on my WTA page. I log my summits on Peakbagger.

Black Peak
October 18, 2026

Central North Cascades - Goode-Logan-Black Area

Day hiked at Black Peak, one of my bucket-list peaks in the North Cascades. A little late considering the season but I still decided to give it a shot. We arrived at the parking lot a little before 7am; the lot was about 80% filled, kinda unexpected since larch season was over and it had just rained/snowed yesterday. But it’s understandable as this was a most sunny Sunday.

We started in the dark with our headlamps on for about 20 minutes, cruising on the trail. Many hikers were already out, but none were aiming for the peak.

The trail up to the boulder fields is straightforward and cruising. Then the trail is covered by big boulders, and morning frost (a sign the dry scramble season is about to end) makes it a little bit tedious. We were just picking our own lines on the way in. On the way out, we found that about 60% of the boulder-hopping section can be totally avoided by staying on climber’s right for a dirt trail; the rest is still find-your-own-line, but staying climber’s right is still less annoying.

Very beautiful views, especially the lakes and the eastern peaks under morning light, along the way to Wing Lake. There were still some gold larches left, mostly smaller trees—better than my last week’s trip to Pasayten.

We filled some water in the stream above Wing Lake; the lake itself is also quite accessible to reach.

Scree slope to the saddle:
Probably the least enjoyable part. The enjoyable parts on the way up are the crossing of the dead glacier remnant (pretty icy with snow crust, but the slope is really gentle) and the view of Wing Lake below. The scree slopes get worse near the top; be cautious when downclimbing. On the way down, I found that staying on climber’s left lets you use some detour and little switchbacks that help lower the steepness a little.

South ridge climb to the summit block:
Very uneventful, mostly class 2 and felt very safe. Little to no slick scree slopes. The views behind are jaw-dropping—the greatest part of this hike!

Summit block:
It’s more sketchy than I expected. After reading some trip reports, I thought it was just yet another class 3 block if I used the most resistant J-hook route by circling around the summit and climbing up to the east. But the technicality of the climbing moves is still more demanding since I have no climbing background. The crux is actually the very first move I encountered, either left to a huge slab with poor footholds, or right to some more blocky pieces but requiring clear spotting of the exact moves. I used the slab up and the right route down. Otherwise, I find this part not as exposed as they said, just technically tricky/demanding.

Spectacular views once we were on the saddle, notably a sea of crevassed North Cascade glaciers and the very prominent Goode Mountain. The summit is a more airy version of this, but you can get 70% of the joy just standing on the saddle. There was a very thin layer of icy snow crust on a few of the summit block rocks—not a big deal for my moves, but I felt somewhat sketchy. The sun may thaw them out in the next few sunny days. I wouldn’t do the summit block anyway if there is more snow—definitely a no-go.

We encountered about 10 groups around the peak; some parties had just finished their climb the day before.

Wild animals:
A few wild ducks spotted on Lewis Lake; pikas are crazily active in the afternoon!

Gears:
We brought microspikes since we were unsure about the frost/fresh snow conditions, but never thought about putting them on. You don’t need them for crossing the gentle snowfield (dead glacier remains).

We finished in 11.5 hours, with about 5,500 ft gain and 13.5 miles logged. A challenging hike, but worth it.

The Two Craggies (and a Late Larch Viewing Trip)
October 11–12, 2026

Okanogan Range - Central Pasayten Area

I day hiked Big Craggy Peak and West Craggy and was the only party on the mountain. Originally I planned to hike Star and Courtney for larches, but my buddy was not available this weekend, so I opted for this other pair of eastern Cascade peaks instead.

The drive from Seattle is truly long—nearly 5 hours one way, since my sedan was not able to drive fast on the unpaved section, which is also very long, like 7–8 miles. As daylight gets shorter these days, I decided to car camp in the lower developed campground along NF5130 the night before. Late fall night was freezing cold, around 28.

NF5130 is passable by any car but is washboarded for about half of the unpaved section. There is one giant pothole, like a pond, close to the trailhead; it can be easily maneuvered around.

I downloaded three GPX files for this trip. All of them leave the Copper Glance Trail and go directly up the relatively gentle ridge of Big Craggy. I didn’t see any obvious boot pack for Big Craggy, but this route needs minimal navigation. The upper rock section has some steep, slippery scree, so be prepared—I didn’t know at the time that this trip is full of such steep, slippery scree/talus slopes. The summit block is easy-peasy, with minimal scramble moves. It’s worth noting that Big Craggy seems to have two similar-height peaks; the USGS one is the east one.

My three GPX tracks also agree on most of the traversing section. The so-called crux of the trip is a gully that is quite obvious; the scrambling is low Class 3 at best, and it is blessed with blocky rock. In my opinion, it’s far better than the scree slopes. At this season, there was a dusting of snow, 1–2 inches, mostly on the west side. Fortunately, the dusting did not affect the gully scramble, so I carried on to West Craggy.

Once on the ridge of West Craggy, I noticed the talus slopes were all dusted with snow. Considering the slope is gentle and it’s talus, not scree, I walked up to the summit. West Craggy is even more of a walk-up compared to its bigger brother. The register can be found under two talus rocks on the west side of the summit platform.

The only unenjoyable part, however, was getting down from West Craggy to the trail—oh, sweet home. Here is where the three GPX tracks disagree. I also saw a fourth GPX option that goes directly down a chossy gully without needing to downclimb the Class 3 gully crux, but it’s also the steepest! I opted for the yellow one. It has less chossy talus slopes, which I think is the most resistant one, but it also needs the most sidehilling back before going down.

I ran all the way down since the Copper Glance Trail is very well graded and has zero obstacles.

No water is available once you pass the very first creek, which is only 20 minutes of hiking from the trailhead. The tarn is pretty dirty, and a better running stream can be found before that, in the valley.

It took me 11.5 hours to do this solo. I’m terrible at downhill steep scree, and I also took some time shooting photos, having lunch at West Craggy, and dinner at the tarn. You may want to use microspikes in later October, but currently careful footwork works. Anyhow, I wouldn’t do this in early winter since the steep downhill from West Craggy is sketchy considering the snow covered slick scree and talus.

The larches appear to have already passed their peak, and the needles are dropping fast. Some are still holding on around Copper Glance Lake and in the valley.

Gilbert Peak Overnight and Stargazing
October 3–4, 2026

Goat Rocks (Highest Point)

We did a weekend overnight at Warm Lake and scrambled up Gilbert Peak the second day. Gilbert Peak is the high point of the Goat Rocks and the tallest nonvolcanic peak in the South Cascades. It combines the region’s unique views, terrain, and landscape, and it appears on both Smoot’s list and Goldman’s scramble list (both versions). The approach from Conrad Meadows is the scenic option and you can choose either a lower camp at Surprise Lake or a high camp at Warm Lake. Scramble routes can either be ridge walking on the Klickton divide or using the Meade glacier (early season).

We were the only backpackers/hikers there, and we had the entire mountain and the trail to Warm Lake to ourselves for the whole weekend (probably others are out for larches, which is not available here). How rare!

Road to the TH is okay for sedans, few big potholes but not hard to maneuver. Drive all the way on NF1200 (paved FR, wow!) and only turn into NF1000 once you see an intersection. Do not follow the navigation which may leads you on NF750 or some rough roads.

The trail to Warm Lake is in supreme condition—possibly the best trail I have ever used to be honest. Very few downed trees, well-graded slopes, and good switchbacks. In general, it is suitable for everyone, albeit very, very long. The biggest highlight is Conrad Meadows, stunning, open with a few fall colors. It would be great if it replaced the long hike through the forest, but it lasts only for the first 2 miles. There are several junctions on route to Warm Lake: one major junction for South Fork Tieton and another later one that I assume splits toward Surprise Lake. It is fairly easy to navigate if you follow a GPX.

The Warm Lake campsite is gorgeous, but the lake itself is no larger than a tarn. The lake is clear and shining blue-green in the sunshine, with striking reflections and shadows of Gilbert Peak and the sharp ridge. There are about six campsites on the other side of the lake, opposite the main trail. The campsite can be very windy, too. Chipmunks are very prevalent there, so bring a bear canister or hang your food. They even jumped on our backpacks while we were busy with our tents nearby.

There are plenty of water sources in the first several miles of the hike, but none until Warm Lake.

From camp, the scramble up the peak still has a pretty clear trail to follow all the way near the summit block. The climbers’ beaten path is fairly easy to spot. If you are on it, it is no more than Class 2. Some times it leads us to the right of the ridge and sometimes to the left. There are some slightly exposed catwalk sections, which is expected since it is a ridge. The alpine plateau on the ridge is wide open and very special.

The summit block is indeed Class 3. Some steep scree ramps could be the hardest part. The scramble is not crazy, though there is a bit of exposure. Aim for the true block on your climber’s left instead of the false one which you come across at first, as there is no nontechnical way to connect them. The summit is cramped and a bit exposed (the sheer drop off of the east face), and the wind is super strong, but once you are up you can admire Rainier, Adams, St. Helens, and the other Goat Rocks peaks. A summit register is available.

There was some new snow, but most had melted. You can see the fresh snow filled up some glacier crevasses and cracks. Meade Glacier is dying, and you can only see it once on the last part of the ridge as its size is probably less than 1/3 of what on the USGS map. Conrad Glacier, on the north face, is in even worse condition. Its decline has been dramatic, with the detachment from Cold Lake and the disappearance of its lower section.

On our way out, we came across a group of hunters on horses.

25 miles, 5,100 ft gain.

Mount Washington (Olympic)
September 27, 2026

Eastern Olympic Mountains - Hamma Hamma-Cushman Group (Highest Point)

This is my first time hiking the Olympic mountains. Rare clear day on a very prominent peak --- the summit of Mt Washington is usually in fog as it sits just above Lake Cushman and Hood Canal. Direct and steep from the start, with class 2–3 scrambling starts about halfway up. Leave the trail, and it gets spicier, we ended up scrambling a dry waterfall only to find out we went slightly sideways left of our gpx track. Stay on the beaten line unless that’s what you want. Muddy and wet rocks in places, but nothing too bad. There is one very long scree ramp that is pretty challenging on the way down. The summit block can be solved in different ways, and we opted for the higher traverse on the climber’s right side, which seems borderline class 4. Other hikers told us that there is a lower route also circling to the right that is basically walking, probably class 2-3. Summit rock is not great; test holds if you go looking for harder moves, or just stay on the normal route (look for those beaten scree ramps). For comparison: my buddy found the summit blocks more exposed than Kaleetan, a bit more technical than Del Campo. Overall steepness felt similar to Snoqualmie mtn, or a little less.

As one of Smoot's list peaks, the views are spectacular, especially on this clear day. Three volcanoes (Rainier, Adams, and MSH) can be seen on your back, and nearly all the Olympic Range shows up when you are at the summit. The range looks pretty snow-free/glacierless compared to the Cascades, exposing brown/red-ish rocks, except for Olympus and Anderson --- still a shock how fast those glaciers and icefields are going!

Summit register available at the top. Easy to find.

Almost no water source along the trail. Very weak stream near 47.52083, -123.24401. No running stream found on the upper mountain.

Low to moderate crowd. We came across 7 parties on a sunny autumn Sunday.

Snowking Mountain Scenic Backpacking
September 18–20, 2026

Central North Cascades - Buckindy-Snowking Area

I did a splendid 3-day backpacking trip (Sep 18 to 20) in the Snowking Mountain area and camped at the gorgeous Cyclone Lake. Originally, I planned for an overnight, but the peaceful lake simply wasn’t enough for the short stay.

Snowking Mountain is one of the very few actively glaciated alpine summits in the central North Cascades region (Ruth Mtn, Tomyhoi, and Prophet are north of SR20, so they don’t count) that don’t require mountaineering skills or advanced scrambling. Maybe the only other peak is Colonial Peak, but that involves some mandatory Class 4, steep snow, and more exposure. Its huge north face is covered with snow, with three spiky peaks; it just looked like a crown from a distance if you viewed it from the north.

The mountain is getting more popular now. I hiked up on a Friday, started around 10 a.m. from my car, and arrived at the campsite a little before 5, at a leisurely pace. I came across at least six parties that day. The campsites are a bit limited, so you wouldn’t want to come very late, especially on a sunny weekend. The road to the trailhead is mostly decent, but I could not drive my RWD sedan with 5 inches of clearance through one dry creek remnant, so I walked an additional 3.2 mi round trip to where the climber's trail starts (not so great), mostly flat. I saw a Subaru Impreza parked at the Cascade River bridge that I think 4WD vehicles could make it over if driven carefully (P.S.: on my way out to my car, I saw a Lexus RX slightly scrape the front when passing that one…). After that, the road became rougher, but still far better than the FR to Hidden Lake Lookout.

Hike to camp:
On the route up to the campsite, you follow a climber’s trail marked with blue/white and orange stripes along the way. The trail is pretty well defined and better than those climbers' trails for rock climbing. The slope is pretty steep, though; you can imagine it as the Mailbox Peak old trail. There are about three “knobs” you need to get past along the way, so tons of ups and downs. As other reports suggest, there is no water at all along the way in the late season, but the good news is the trail is shaded. I thought there were several locations where one should be cautious. Be sure to turn climber's right when you intersect with the old logging road (if you encounter too many overgrown trees and shrubs, then you must be going the wrong way); there are some sections that are less defined just before ascending Found Peak, the biggest knob; and turn climber's left so you don’t accidentally walk on the Found Lake Trail. Also, there is some gentle bushwhacking along the way, so wear pants if you mind. I would say the rough trail actually makes the hike in the old growth more enjoyable, and once you reach Found Peak and later on Kindy Ridge, the view just opens up magically—on your right is the shiny Snowking Mountain; on your left and behind you, wow, Eldorado, Forbidden, Boston, Jburg, Formidable, and the Ptarmigan traverse peaks-all the Crown Jewels of the core North Cascades are just showing up like a dream :)

Campsite:
Campsites are all located near the lower tarn and Cyclone Lake. I picked one place that turned out to be the closest to the lake. Use this gpx if you want: 48.41763, -121.25532. Water in the tarns didn’t look fresh, so I opted for the lake. The access was not very easy; you need a bit of scrambling down to get there. Slippers are probably not a good choice. Cyclone Lake is unbelievably clear, and you can see the bedrock clearly. Small fish, frogs, and other wildlife can be spotted there. The water temperature is a bit chilly but not freezing cold; one can try swimming there, while some brave dude cliff-jumped into the lake. I spent one of the most enjoyable sunset moments by the lake. For food storage, I carried a BearVault with me, but this place seems to have no bears at all (no berries along the way), and lots of pine trees made hanging your food possible.

Scramble Snowking:
The mountain itself is a straightforward ridge scramble all the way to the top. Interestingly, there is a rather clear trail you can follow from the camp to halfway up the climb. The boulders along the way are very solid and seldom shift. There are some very huge boulders, and you can just walk on them because the slope is rather gentle. I did encounter two or three sections that require some downclimbing or borderline Class 4 moves, but these can always be done with more resistant moves if you spend time on route finding. Don’t risk it if you are not comfortable. The summit block is not scary. The rock quality is less solid than the ridge but not bad. I carried a helmet with me but never put on or felt it was needed—most of the parties I came across never brought one. Once on the summit, you will find a flat place near a beautiful cairn that can host a small party. Looking to your left, you can appreciate the huge Snowking Glacier on the north face, with the Middle and East Peaks standing out. In general, in terms of scrambling, I find this peak more demanding than Mt. Daniel and a little bit more than Dragontail. There is a rather flat snowfield (stay on your left; avoid moving too far away, or you won’t be on the glacier) that you can use to save your legs from continuous scrambling. No gear at all is required for crossing the snowfield. You can summit the peak 100% dry if you want.

Hike out:
Be careful about the steep dirt and muddy sections; they can be treacherous. The descent is long, but with all the good memories, you won’t be too sucked.

Be sure to take some photos of the remaining North Cascades classic peaks once you are in the alpine zone!

Little Devil of Cascade River😈
August 31, 2026

Central North Cascades - Eldorado Massif

"Back again for the third time at Monogram Lake TH. That's the spirit!"

Haven't had a big trip for about a month, so I headed for Little Devil Peak in the Teebone ridge area (got its name for looking like a T-bone steak I suppose) on a relatively clear and sunny day. Teebone ridge has a series of lesser-known, moderately elevated but glaciated alpine peaks on the western border of central north cascades, also bordering NCNP. Little Devil is the second-highest peak on the ridge and stands out as a central point with Baksit.

Little Devil is one of the non-technical C2C peaks with everything packed - a strenuous hike to approach, boulder hopping, off-trail road finding through gorgeous subalpine meadows, glacier crossing, many scramble opportunities, and scenic views along the way of the North cascades once you are on the Teebone ridge.

There are three main climbing trails used to lead you to the summit: (a) head directly to the teebone ridge and traverse to Devil's benchmark, then scramble the "cliff" connecting to Little Devil; (b) stay at the contour below the ridge, sidehilling all the way to the glacier basin, then cross the glacier and scramble to the summit; and (c) follow the Monogram lake trail all the way to the lake, then sidehilling from the other side and join route (b) once arrive at the glacier basin. This time I chose route (b) for it avoiding some necessary and highly exposed class 4 moves required by (a) and also being more direct than (c) if you are not camping by the lake. It turns out the prolonged sidehilling is no joke either. You have to consistently traverse 40-45 heather and shrub slopes (no trees, fortunately). A slip is normal, especially in the morning; the dew makes them rather slippery. Moreover, the route is not that straightforward; you have to go up and down consistently, depending on the exact drop-offs and steep areas - tons of route-finding involved!

Finally, after crossing some boulders (also steep slopes!), you will find yourself immersed in a totally different alpine zone with granite and a small glacier, which is the highlight of the trip. You have to literally "drop" yourself to the glacier because it has lost its height through the years, which exposes cliff bands (not shown on caltopo). For dropping yourself down, there are two ways: go down a steep permanent snowfield connecting the flat glacier, or scramble/down climb some boulders. I opted for the second and put on my crampons when I arrived at relative flat glacier basin. Be sure to wear your helmet because the rock falling from the cliff created by the disappearing glacier is a real thing, and stay away from sloped glacier borders asap!!

Glacier: it is a rather benign dead glacier, but not a snowfield. There are some small crevasses, and some moulins can swallow you for sure. So going here when it's still covered in winter snow is perhaps not 100% safe. I would say it's riskier than some other dead glacier like the Colchuck glacier. I stayed slightly to the east as recommended in other reports.

Summit block: the actual summit scramble feels secure and moderately exposed. Boulders are very high quality, just like Enchantments area rocks. The harder part, actually, is the short section where you leave the very north edge part of the glacier (the moat is manageable, but it's quick-melting; be sure not to step on the snow; always use solid ice) and scramble a steep ramp of scree. A slip wouldn't be pleasant here, of course! It's more resistant to stay on the east side, where there are relatively larger rocks to break if you slip.

Wildlife: I spotted 3 bears (2 black and 1 brownish) during my trip, all during the Teebone ridge slope traversing. The brownish bear made eye contact with me for a while and ran off quickly and vigilantly.

Other trip highlight: Lots of Vaccinium ovalifolium (ovalleaf blueberry/huckleberry) once you are crossing the subalpine meadow, they grow just along the trails (lower altitude), along with Vaccinium deliciosum (Cascade dwarf blueberry) all the way up to the ridge. I actually find the former tastier since it reaches a perfect balance of acidity and sweetness ("Zesty") and is far more juicy on your palate. Highly recommend! I picked some in the lower trail section because higher up the ridge is bear territory - be sure to share the treat with them.

Water sources (late season):

1. One big creek just before the intersection of lookout mountain lookout and monogram lake trail. This is the most reliable water source so be sure to fill up;

2. Some water while traversing the ridge, would not trust them in late summer/autumn;

3. Snowmelt streams available once you leave the glacier and start scrambling up.

Gears:

  • (Used) Trail runners, 10-point walking crampons w/ flexible center links, poles, helmet (a must if you opt for crossing the glacier), and ice axe
  • (Not used) N/A
  • Comment: Microspikes could work, but I would not rely on them. An ice axe is perhaps not required for a late-season trip like mine.

Cannon Mountain
August 3, 2026

Wenatchee Mountains - Stuart Range

My first real bushwhacking experience with some unrelated and unplanned drama :)

Routes:

Route up (NW "bushwacking" ridge climb, similar to this: https://www.peakbagger.com/climber/ascent.aspx?aid=3239936): I started from Stuart Lake TH and hiked 1.4mi then turned off-trail and went SE direction, directly up the ridge line after crossed the lower forest, a wide boulder field and some bushwacking. The final SE ridgeline is unforested and of high quality boulders with 3 challenging blocks that can either be solved creatively with class 2-3 solutions or more technical ways.

Route down (https://www.mountaineers.org/activities/routes-places/cannon-mountain-southeast-approach): Down climbed the summit pyrimid. Descended SE on the plateau and went down a relatively steep and choss albeit short gully down the ridgeline connecting Enchantments Peak HP. Then descended further SE across two shallow ponds, gained some elevation to Prusik Pass, and went down following the thru-hiking trail.

Comparison of routes: Route up is steeper (4700ft in 2.4miles once you are off trail) and more bushwhacking, came with a longer scramble section for 1/3 of the route, and only about 3.9mi from Stuart Lake TH to the summit block; route down is more scenic and easy technically but much much longer (~10mi from Stuart Lake TH and ~12mi from Snow Lake TH) from either of the two trailheads (That means its very tiring if you use this route both ascent and descent). For climbing the summit pyrimid, starting from the North and traverse south on the short exposed ridgeline or direcly climb up from the south if you started from the plateau.

Fun and moderately exposed scramble on the cost of bushwhacking. Some parts of the climb are definitely more than class 2+ from some sources. The upside is that rocks are solid even among those in the Enchantment. The view of the mountain itself is a little bit underwhelming—particularly due to its being rather forested till the top, lack of jaw-dropping granite walls/spires, and lack of snow coverage early on.

Won’t do it again and can only recommend Cannon's bushwhacking route to those who enjoy pure wilderness exploration and collecting Bulger peaks.

Putrid Pete's Peak and Mount Defiance Traverse
July 19, 2026

Alpine Lakes Area - Snoqualmie Pass North

Went a light day for traversing Putrid Pete’s Peak (P3) and Mt Defiance in the nearby I-90 area. 10 miles, 4800ft gain for 7.5 hours (4.5 hours moving), pretty good work out and light scrambing+fun route finding.

We followed the route from AllTrails and it is mostly accurate. Putrid Pete’s peak is mostly just steep trails, with one necessary boulder cross and a final class 2 summit pyramid finish—the very top is probably with 1 or 2 class 3 moves.

Then the fun part of today was traversing on the ridge. There’s definitely some route finding required although you can nearly always spot multiple faint climber trails. Stay on the very top of the ridge as much as you can and sometimes faint climber trails will lead you temporarily on the north of the ridge that’s okay. Most of the hard part are in the first 1/3 of the traversing.

Mt Defiance is a very easy dirt trail. Lots of flies, bees, beetles and butterflies on top of the peak! Good place for a summit panorama. Lots of wildflowers along the way to the summit. Be careful to hike with caution when going down some of the steep dirt sections.

On a sunny sunday, the Ira Springs parking lot filled up before 9. But very few people on this route. We came across 3 parties on P3, and we are the only party on the ridge traverse.

Ruth Mountain
June 11, 2026

Skagit Range - Baker-Shuksan Massif

"With the fresh snow and sunny skies, Ruth Mountain simply delivered the best alpine views I have ever seen. A totally unforgettable experience in the white!"

I headed here on a sunny day with great weather. Very stunning view of the surrounding mountains once you pass point 5930 on a clear day.

Highlights:

A manageable, fully-packed real alpine traveling experience with top notch views.

Conditions:

The snow is very soft once you are on the Ruth arm. Snowpacks are actively collapsing thank to the heat and sunshine — multiple fresh wet slides noticed (and an agile marmot was spotted!). The most sketchy part I would say is the traversing point 5930 since the slope is steep and a slide would be somewhat consequential — I moved extremely slow to prevent side slide when I’m back at noon, be prepared to self arrest and keep alerted. The snow is continued to be in such shape with only a bit being slightly better. Postholing to low to mid thigh even in the morning and a bit more when back.

The other sketchy part in this season is the steep (max 38 degree according to caltopo) ridge climb to point 5930, snow is fine in the morning but it gets really annoying in the afternoon since it’s slushy outside and firm inside (mashed potatoes) makes plunge steps awkward, French techniques impossible and down climb unnecessary. I spent like years coming down and decided just to glissaded down after taking off the crampons and lock into a runway.

It was surprising I only met two other parties on Ruth on such a beautiful day, with the solo climber we ascended together and it was a great experience. The other party are bc skiers, seems we all made to the top with no troubles.

Prediction and before you go:

Giving the weather condition, the snowpack on the arm is sure to continue worsen and even now maybe it’s on the tipping point. One should access their snow traveling skills and risk tolerance especially on potential slides and crevasse falls due to the unstable snow and expect it to continue melt in the following weeks. Be ready to turn back anywhere on the steep ridge climb, on the 5930 traversing, or before the hidden glacier beneath. Come again in late summer if you are not willing to take the risk but you will lose all the alpine, solitude feel at the cost. No open crevasses spotted during my trip and I didn’t even notice the glacier toe due to the thick snow coat.

Tools I used

Normal mountaineering equipment including a walking axe, rigid boots, crampons (needed for the morning ice ridge climb, optional for the arm to summit part), helmet for extra protection if falling.

Bring plenty of water from the hannegan camp as postholing all the way under the sun is killing you. I used up my 2.5 liters on the mountain and would wish I actually brought 4.

Given all the evidence spotted, wet slides, postholing… although the snow is there, but it behaves like late summer condition due to the warm winter. This is not a normal early season experience you should expected (no freeze-thaw cycles at all despite mountain weather report one…). Soloing now is highly risky and not advisable as the snow is losing their structural force.

Hidden Lake Lookout/Peaks (Winter Climber's Trail) Attempt
May 23, 2026

Central North Cascades - Eldorado Massif

Unexpected, tough adventure for my buddy's birthday. We started driving around 3 from the Newhalem campground, where we arrived the day before for this weekend. My buddy is currently not prepared for such an early-season, off-trail hike, so he actually bought a brand-new ice axe for this outing (turns out he loves the tool very much, more than trekking poles!). My Lexus could not make it up once leaving the Cascade River Road. The forest road is infamous for being the most "backcountry" road in this region, according to someone who worked here. We parked and had to walk the extra miles. It turned out that not only my car, but also my buddy's Mazda SUV would not likely make it either due to the crazy "wheel trails" carved along the dirt road.

This was just the first challenge. We knew in advance that the forest road was washed out due to winter storms, so there might be some extra challenges. Soon we had to cross an icy cold, rather turbulent creek. My buddy was adventurous and went across quickly. I, always being a bit more cautious, decided to wade through barefoot first - also to protect my thick socks inside the mountaineering boots (a stupid choice since I originally wanted to use the "hike" to break them in, but what turns out to be next is rather painful for my feet). But the water is so cold that soon I noticed it wouldn't be an option. I put my socks on and booted up again, stabilizing myself using my poles, and managed to cross it nervously, as my mountaineering boots are not flexible and have about zero grip on wet stones, not to mention that those are submerged in the turbulent creek. I wasted at least 15 minutes here.

A bigger challenge ahead is the notoriously steep and wild winter "trail"-it's not actually a trail, as the route is not well defined, and we encountered multiple bushwhacking sections at hip height. Fortunately, the terrain is mostly soft, rotten root/vegetarian systems, so far better than moving up a 35-degree muddy slope. Also, we were not super good at route-finding, so sometimes we were not at the ridge and needed to regain it.

We finally broke through the forest and saw snow for the first time. We tried to avoid them as much as possible, as my buddy only wore a pair of normal hiking boots - not ideal for kicking steps in those moderate snow slopes. Soon we found ourselves facing a mixture of huge boulders and iffy snow covering them. There seemed to be no detour to this boulder field. We originally planned on going a little higher up to be closer to a more alpine, snowy view, if we made it to the top. As I tested one of the very first snow bridges while we attempted to move higher, it collapsed. That was the time I knew we probably should call it a day - as I still have another trip planned (turns out to be a mild disaster), and it was way below our schedule. We had some delicious sandwiches and took some pictures before heading down.

On the way down, it sucked. Sometimes we dropped too far sideways and missed the ridge, so we had to reclimb. It wasn't enjoyable for my buddy, and he talked less and less when we were moving down. We had to take more frequent rests. At one of the stops, we heard something suspicious - looking up, we found ourselves encountering a widowmaker: a tree was swaying in the wind and at risk of falling down on top of us. We ran away as quickly as possible, me barely grabbing my stuff.

It was unlucky that my buddy got his big toe hurt quite badly during the brutal hike down. I was really proud of him for still making it down and even hiked for a while once we were back on the forest road (that was REAL hope!). We finally hitchhiked a lovely family's "adventure van" down to my sedan. They planned to check this hike out but turned back due to the creek crossing (the water runs even harder in the afternoon). The driver maneuvered his van quite masterfully, and I learned that he used to do lots of mountaineering stuff when he was younger, with his brother. He has long since quit the hobby, while his brother continued. I also learned that Ruth Mountain is a very good peak for glacier climbing, as it's "not crevassed," so often used as a training ground for aspiring climbers. We chatted about outdoor stuff soon before we were brought to my car.

We had no rest before I rushed to drive my buddy to the campsite, and decided to drive back again for the planned Monogram Lake overnight, though it would be dark soon. What a bold move that turned out to be too ambitious for a tired body (I was hesitant when I put on the quite heavy overnight backpack on my shoulder, even thought about turning back when I started the first few hundred feet of the hike to the lake, but something inside me pushed me up).

Overall, quite an adventurous and tough day for both of us!
